Colored Gemstones

Le Vian prices 17 antique-cushion styles from $1,499.99

The cut arrives at Kay Jewelers in mid-October across eight natural gemstones, from nude quartz to rose morganite. The in-store range tops out at $4,999.99, with further designs online.

By The Coloured Stones Desk

10 October 2026

What changed

A cut goes national at Kay. Le Vian priced its Antique Cushion Cut collection for Kay Jewelers at $1,499.99 to $4,999.99 across 17 in-store styles, arriving mid-October. It uses eight natural gemstones rather than diamonds.

What it means

  • If you buy for a mass-market case: A chain is selling a cut as the reason to buy, opening at four figures.
  • If you sell antique-cut diamonds: The hand-cut argument is now being made with quartz, not diamond.
  • What this is not: A carat-weight disclosure. None is published.

Seventeen styles, four figures

Fifteen hundred up. The in-store range runs $1,499.99 to $4,999.99 across 17 styles, in Kay locations from mid-October, with further designs online.

Two published examples. An oval-cut diamond ring in 14-carat honey gold is listed at $3,499.99 and an aquamarine and diamond ring at $1,949.99.

Three and a third. The top of the in-store range is 3.33 times the bottom. That is a mass-market ladder rather than a fine-jewellery one.

Eight stones, no diamonds

Quartz leads the palette. The collection uses nude, mint, honey and chocolate quartz, sky and deep sea blue topaz, sea aquamarine and rose morganite: eight natural gemstones.

Chosen for colour. Le Vian says the eight were selected for colours that evoke the palette of rare natural fancy colour diamonds. No price for those diamonds is published.

No weights given. No carat weight is published for any stone. The collection is positioned by cut and colour rather than by size.

The cut is the argument

Four stated features. Le Vian describes a larger facet, a linear culet and broad crown facets. It adds longer mirrored reflections from pavilion step cuts, and a hidden halo in key pieces.

Vintage was forecast. Le Vian's 2027 forecast centred on personal style and vintage, including natural gemstones cut in the manner of old mine diamonds.

Against lab-grown. JCK attributes the antique-cut trend partly to hand-cut irregularity that separates such stones from lab-grown material, and partly to celebrity wear.

What would change this call

Carat weights. A collection priced by cut with no size disclosure is a marketing position rather than a measurable one, and published weights could make the same prices read as either generous or thin. It would also read differently if the online assortment is priced materially above or below the in-store range, which Le Vian has not published.

Why a cut can be a mass-market product

A modern round brilliant is engineered for light return and is reproducible to a tolerance, which is exactly why it is hard to sell as a differentiator: every retailer has it and a lab-grown stone cut the same way is optically indistinguishable to a shopper. Antique cuts behave differently. An old mine or old European cut was finished by hand against a different optical objective, with a large open culet, fewer and broader facets and a chunkier crown, and the result reads as warmer flashes rather than the fine scintillation of a modern round. That irregularity is the selling point, because it is a look rather than a specification and therefore cannot be undercut purely on price per carat. Applying it to coloured gemstones rather than to diamonds is the commercially interesting move here. Quartz, topaz, aquamarine and morganite are abundant and inexpensive relative to diamond, so the cut has to carry the value proposition on its own, and the cost of cutting a broad-facet antique style into mid-hardness material is low enough to keep a four-figure retail price intact. What that buys a chain is a reason to buy that a lab-grown round cannot answer, at a price point a lab-grown round competes at directly.
