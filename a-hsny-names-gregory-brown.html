Watches

HSNY names Gregory Brown its executive director

The society founded in 1866 hands its education and preservation work to a member of ten years. He succeeds Nicholas Manousos, who led there for more than a decade. Carolina Navarro stays as deputy.

By Carat Capital

18 October 2026 Corrected

What changed

A member of ten years takes the chair. The Horological Society of New York has appointed Gregory Brown executive director. He succeeds Nicholas Manousos, who held leadership roles there for more than a decade. Carolina Navarro continues as deputy director.

What it means

  • If you teach or fund watchmaking: Brown oversees the society's education and preservation work. That includes aid to individuals, grants to watchmaking schools and support for independent watchmakers.
  • If you exhibit or speak in New York: The society says it plans larger-scale programmes bringing watch-industry figures together for discussions about horology. No date or venue is named.
  • What this is not: A change of direction announced with a figure. No budget, programme size or membership number accompanies the appointment.

A decade of membership first

The succession is named. Brown succeeds Nicholas Manousos, who held leadership roles at the society for more than a decade. Carolina Navarro, acting director during the transition, continues as deputy.

His background is nonprofit. Brown has more than two decades building entrepreneurial and nonprofit organisations and has been a society member since 2016.

Two horological credentials. He belongs to the Academy of the Grand Prix d'Horlogerie de Geneve. He also covered Baselworld and other trade events for TimeZone.

Education is the job

Programmes come first. Brown will oversee the society's education and preservation work. He is also to widen its role in watchmaking abroad.

The society funds. Founded in 1866 by working watchmakers, it gives aid to individuals and grants to watchmaking schools. It also backs independents and runs classes and monthly lectures.

A library behind it. It runs the Jost Burgi Research Library. The collection holds more than 25,000 items on horological scholarship and history.

What would change this call

A figure appearing. This is an appointment at a nonprofit with no published budget, no membership count and no programme spend, so the article reports an office changing hands and nothing about the resources behind it. If the society publishes its programme spend or its membership, the significance of the post becomes measurable and this piece will read as having understated or overstated it. There is no price here to be wrong about.

Why a society's executive director matters to a watch trade

The Horological Society of New York is not a trade association and does not represent brands, which is what makes the post it has just filled unusual in a business where most institutions are funded by the companies they speak for. It was founded in 1866 by working watchmakers, and the things it does now are the things a trade cannot easily do for itself: grants to the schools that train watchmakers, financial aid to individuals, support for independents who have no marketing department, classes, monthly lectures, and a research library of more than 25,000 items. The constraint on all of it is a small institution's capacity, which is why the executive director's job is substantially a fundraising and programme-building one rather than a curatorial one. The succession matters for a practical reason. American watchmaking has a documented training shortage, and the pipeline runs through a handful of schools whose intake is partly a function of who funds scholarships. An organisation that both grants to those schools and convenes the industry is one of the few places where that pipeline can be widened without a brand's permission. The society says the new director will work to expand its role internationally and to build larger-scale programmes, and has attached no figure to either ambition, so the test is whether the programmes appear rather than whether a target is met.
