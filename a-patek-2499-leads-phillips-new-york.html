Watches

Patek 2499 in pink gold leads Phillips at over $3m

The 5 and 6 December sale in New York carries a second-series pink gold example. Phillips says eight are publicly known of 349 made, and seven named lots carry a $6.3 million floor.

By Carat Capital

17 October 2026

What changed

Phillips names its December leader. A second-series Patek Philippe Reference 2499 in pink gold leads The New York Watch Auction: XV on 5 and 6 December. Phillips estimates it above $3 million and says eight such examples are publicly known.

What it means

  • If you collect perpetual chronographs: Phillips puts the second-series pink gold population at eight, against 349 examples of the reference in total. That is its count, not an independent census.
  • If you bid on independents: Three F.P. Journe lots carry floors of $600,000, $600,000 and above $500,000. One is described as the only known pink gold Pre-Souscription Resonance.
  • What this is not: A result or a record. Phillips' claim to the highest-value US watch catalogue is a pre-sale claim about its own consignments.

The leader and its population

One reference, two counts. Phillips says 349 examples of the Reference 2499 were made over nearly 35 years and that eight second-series examples in pink gold are publicly known.

Both are the house's. Carat Capital has not matched either count against a registry or a maker's record. They are repeated here as Phillips' statements about its own leading lot.

The estimate is open. The watch is given as more than $3 million with no upper figure. An open-ended estimate sets a floor and declines to set a ceiling.

Independents fill the middle

Journe takes three slots. A second-generation Tourbillon Souverain and a pink gold Pre-Souscription Resonance each carry $600,000 to $1.2 million. An Octa Reserve de Marche prototype is above $500,000.

Akrivia joins them. A unique AK-02 Tourbillon carries $500,000 to $1 million, which puts a contemporary independent in the same bracket as the vintage Patek's supporting lots.

A gem-set Patek follows. Reference 5971 with 42 baguette-cut emeralds on the bezel and seven emerald hour markers is estimated at $600,000 to $1.2 million.

Two lots are not wristwatches

Farouk's clock returns. A Cartier Model A Mystery Clock commissioned by King Farouk I in 1936 and delivered the following year is expected above $500,000.

Seinfeld's Daytona too. The Vic Elford Rolex Daytona from Jerry Seinfeld's collection was announced earlier and carries no published estimate in this report.

A charity section runs. The third TimeForArt edition sits inside the sale with the Swiss Institute taking all proceeds, including the house's donated buyer's premium.

What to watch

  • Before 5 DecemberA full catalogue with a lot count. Seven lots are named and the sale's size is not published.
  • 5-6 DecemberWhether the Reference 2499 clears $3 million. The estimate is a floor with no stated ceiling.
  • 5-6 DecemberWhether the sale total supports Phillips' pre-sale claim to the highest-value US watch auction.

What would change this call

The catalogue itself. Everything here comes from one trade report of a house's own pre-sale announcement, and no lot has sold. Phillips' two population counts for the Reference 2499 are the softest claims in the piece: a count of publicly known examples is by construction a count of what the counter knows, and a ninth pink gold second series could surface in the sale room. The reporting outlet also discloses that its first draft was produced with AI assistance before staff review, which is a reason to hold each figure to the catalogue when it appears.

Why an open-ended estimate is a different instrument

Most auction estimates come as a range, a low and a high, and the pair does two jobs at once. The low is a signal to bidders about where the reserve sits, because a reserve is conventionally at or below it. The high is a signal about where the house expects competition to stop. When a house publishes an estimate as more than a figure, it is deliberately doing only the first job. It establishes a floor, usually to reassure a consignor and to filter the room, and it declines to guess at a ceiling because it does not want the published high to become one. Three of the seven named lots in this catalogue are presented that way: the Patek at more than $3 million, the Journe prototype and the Cartier clock each at more than $500,000. For a reader trying to size the sale, the practical consequence is that no total can be built from these numbers. A sum of the published lows is a hard floor, which is why this paper prints $6.3 million and calls it exactly that. A sum of the highs cannot be built at all, because three of them do not exist. That asymmetry is not a reporting gap; it is the house's choice, and it is worth noticing which lots it applies to. Here it applies to the leader and to the two most difficult-to-comparable objects in the sale, a prototype and a royal commission, which are precisely the lots with the thinnest comparable record.
