The Lab-Grown Diamond Price ListWhat a made diamond costs when nothing about it is scarce

Lab-grown prices do not behave like natural ones. The per-carat line is flat, the fancy-shape discount has inverted, and the retail spread is wider than anything on the natural counter. Every figure below is dated, sourced, and marked with the basis it stands on.

A one-carat round lab-grown diamond asks $545 per carat against $4,581 for its natural counterpart (StoneAlgo shape sample, 25 Jul 2026) — 88.1% less, a multiple of 8.4. The published wholesale band for the same stone runs $350 to $600 for CVD material (Lepdo, re-confirmed byte-identical and unchanged 2026-09-21). The cheapest tracked retail asking price, $281 at Ritani, sits below the middle of that band. The most expensive, $796 at Brilliant Earth, is 2.8 times the cheapest for an identical specification — both figures re-knocked 2026-09-21 against Rings.com's fresh 18 September 2026 edition, which supersedes the 30 August edition entirely (251,098 listings across seven retailers, collected 17 September 2026). That edition also re-published a 1ct Market Median for the first time since June: $571, across the four retailers with continuous 1ct coverage.

01

Weight — the line that does not rise

Figure CC/2026/154

Natural diamonds get more expensive per carat as they get bigger, because big rough is rare. Lab-grown diamonds do not: the per-carat price is flat from half a carat to five, moving between $514 and $631 across the whole ladder. Everything that follows is a consequence of that one line.

Why the line is flat

Across a tenfold range of weight the lab-grown per-carat price moves 23%. Across the same range the natural price moves 983%. The gap between the two is therefore not a constant discount but a widening one: 4.5× at half a carat, 9.4× at one (natural side corrected 2026-09-15, James Allen dropped from the retailer set), 55.4× at five. Buying big is where the saving actually lives.

The blank row

The 4.00 ct row carries no natural comparison because the natural sample at that weight was too thin to publish and stays blank on the natural list. The lab figure stands alone rather than against a number we would not print.

02

Shape — the ranking flips

Figure CC/2026/155

In natural diamonds every fancy shape is cheaper than a round, because rounds waste the most rough and rough is the cost. In lab-grown there is no rough to waste: the cost is growing time and cutting loss on a crystal that can be grown to order. At one carat the ranking flips. A lab princess asks 23.5% more than a lab round; a natural princess asks 39.4% less. That is a 62.9-point swing on the same two shapes.

Read this before quoting a premium

The inversion is a one-carat phenomenon, and we will not pretend otherwise. At two carats princess and cushion fall back below round; at three carats six of nine shapes do. Only oval carries a premium at all three weights we track. Anyone quoting a single lab-grown shape premium across all sizes is quoting something that does not exist.

Conflict on the record

Three consumer guides published this year state the opposite of the live data. Liori (27 March) puts emerald cuts at “30–35% savings compared to rounds” and ovals at 20–25% less. Beyond Carat (15 June) prints oval 15–25% less, cushion 20–30% less, emerald 20–30% less, princess 10–20% less. The live one-carat sample has emerald 21.5% above round, cushion 14.3% above, princess 23.5% above. The guidance is the natural-market ordering, copied across to a market that does not work that way.

03

Colour and clarity — the ladder nobody publishes

No source we track publishes a dollar-denominated colour or clarity ladder for lab-grown diamonds from live inventory. We are not going to model one. What the gap still tells you

The modal lab-grown buyer takes D colour and VVS1 clarity. The modal natural buyer takes G and VS. Nobody trades down in a market where the top grade costs almost nothing extra — which is the clearest thing anyone has published about the lab colour and clarity ladder, even though it is not a price.

04

How it was grown — CVD against HPHT

HPHT material carries a premium over CVD at every band published — between 14.5% and 24.0% on band midpoints. The premium is a production fact, not a quality claim: HPHT rigs cost more to run per carat and the two methods yield different colour distributions before treatment. This is the one price structure in lab-grown that has no analogue on the natural counter at all.

05

The counters — same stone, four prices

Fig. CC/2026/156

Same stone, same paper, four counters. At one carat the most expensive asking price is 2.83 times the cheapest, down from 3.84 in the August edition — Ritani raised its 1ct median 26% this edition ($223 to $281), reversing an August decline, while Brilliant Earth's 1ct median eased 7%. At two carats the spread widened to 4.72 times. On the natural counter the equivalent one-carat spread is 2.19 times (five counters, after the 2026-09-15 James Allen correction). Rings.com also re-published a 1ct Market Median this edition ($571, across Ritani/Grown Brilliance/With Clarity/Brilliant Earth) for the first time since June — the 1.5ct and 2ct medians remain withheld under the source's own publication rule (a cell's median only prints when every contributing retailer also had a published figure the prior edition). Clean Origin returned to the panel with n=20 (still below the 30-listing minimum at 1ct; it prints at 1.5ct/2ct, $263/$279 per carat, which this table's four-counter schema does not carry).

The floor is below wholesale

The cheapest tracked asking price at one carat, $281, sits below the midpoint of the published CVD wholesale band for the same specification ($350–600). Either the wholesale guide is stale, or that counter is selling at or below trade. We print both figures and let the gap stand.

Rough turns first

Rough is moving the other way. Chinese producers raised prices at least 30% in recent weeks and Diamond Foundry by about 25%. Polished has not followed yet. If it does, the one-carat round will not be the only riser for long.

Where the saving went

The discount has not widened because natural got dearer. Natural is roughly where it was. Lab fell out from under it — and buyers spent the difference on size, taking the average lab centre stone from 1.31 ct to 2.45 ct, up 87.0% in six years. The flat per-carat line makes that trade almost free.

Source — BriteCo lab-grown vs natural report, data 1 March 2019 to 31 May 2025 · 2026 column, Carat Capital

What a jeweller will pay you

A jeweller will not buy a lab-grown diamond back at any price, because they can order a new one at wholesale tomorrow. The absolute loss is still smaller than on a natural stone — losing all of $2,000 beats losing half of $20,000 — but the retention rate is the one number in this category that has not fallen, because it started at zero.

Excluded

Labrilliante publishes 30–40% retention for lab-grown. It contradicts every trade buyback account we could source and we have not used it.

09

What moved the numbers — the market behind the table

M—01

Henan makes the market

China produces roughly half the world's synthetic diamond output and about 80% of the Chinese total comes out of Henan province, predominantly by HPHT. India is the largest CVD producer at close to a quarter of global output. The newest comparable production breakdown we could source dates to December 2023 and we flag it as such.

M—02

Rough turns first

Chinese producers raised rough prices by at least 30% in recent weeks; Diamond Foundry by about 25%. Polished has not followed. Every previous turn in this market started at the reactor, not the counter.

M—03

A cost signal, not a demand signal

The trade reads the fall as capacity scaling rather than weakening demand — the compression is happening at the producer and migrating down the chain. Lab-grown still took 47.7% of the engagement-ring market in 2024.

M—04

The one-carat round holds

Up 1% year on year against an index down 13%. It is the most liquid item in the category and the only line in the Q2 wholesale list that rose.

M—05

Size absorbs the saving

The average lab centre stone went from 1.31 ct in 2019 to 2.45 ct in 2025, up 87.0%. A flat per-carat curve means going bigger costs almost nothing, so buyers go bigger.

M—06

No list to withhold

Rapaport publishes no lab-grown price list. On the natural page we declined to reconstruct a copyrighted matrix; here there is no matrix to reconstruct, and no benchmark holding the retail range together either.

10

The apparatus — method, sources, exclusions

Method

Two bases, never merged

Retail asking prices come from live inventory indices. Wholesale comes from a published trade guide quoting bands. They are printed as separate figures and never averaged into a single number.

Bands, not midpoints

We show wholesale as the band the source published. Midpoints appear only where we compare CVD against HPHT, because that comparison is internal to one table and one source.

No ladder we cannot source

Colour and clarity stay unpriced. A modelled ladder set beside observed figures reads as data, and we would rather print the gap.

One spec, many counters

The dispersion table holds colour, clarity, shape, cut and lab constant across every retailer, so the range is a pricing difference and not a specification difference.

What we left out

  • Draco 2020–2026 seriesmixes direct-to-consumer tennis-bracelet rates with per-carat averages, so the years are not comparable. Read for direction only, not printed as prices.
  • Washington Diamond price chartquotes one carat at $759–1,500, above every live index we track, and runs a 2025 chart title over a March 2026 date.
  • Labrilliante resale retention30–40% retention contradicts every trade buyback account we could source.
  • Liori and Beyond Carat shape guidanceboth print the natural-market ordering for lab-grown shapes. Shown on this page as a conflict, never used as prices.
  • StoneAlgo 3 ct disagreementthe dedicated 3 ct index page read $1,805 in the same week the summary table read $1,894. Both are shown where relevant; neither is averaged.
