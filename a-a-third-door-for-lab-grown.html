Jean Dousset opens a third store, and it sells only lab-grown

The Cartier descendant's lab-grown-only brand opened at Westfield Valley Fair in Santa Clara on 13 August, after Los Angeles and New York. Price points are not disclosed. The address is the argument.

By The Retail & Technology Desk

21 August 2026

What changed

Jean Dousset opened a third store on 13 August, at Westfield Valley Fair in Santa Clara, California, after a Los Angeles flagship and a New York showroom. The brand has sold lab-grown diamonds exclusively since 2023, across engagement rings, eternity bands, necklaces, bracelets, studs and hoops.

What it means

  • The interesting number here is three, not the lineage. Lab-grown retail has mostly been a volume business run at falling prices through online channels and mall kiosks, and the segment nobody has yet proven is the one that charges a premium for a manufactured stone and defends it with design.
  • Dousset is running that experiment slowly, in expensive addresses, with a surname that opens doors most founders have to knock on. Three stores in three years is not an expansion anybody should extrapolate from.
  • It is, however, the version of lab-grown retail that survives if the stone itself keeps getting cheaper, because it was never selling the stone.

A third door, and where it is

Three doors is not a chain and the location is the argument: Westfield Valley Fair is a top-tier American luxury mall, and it sits in the middle of Silicon Valley rather than in a jewellery district.

The founder is the great-great-grandson of Louis-François Cartier, which is the fact every piece of coverage leads with and the one doing the most commercial work.

A lab-grown-only brand's central problem is legitimacy at a price point where the customer is choosing to spend real money on a stone this trade spent a decade calling a substitute.

An inherited name is the cheapest available answer to that problem, and the brand uses it explicitly: the Santa Clara boutique carries a screen beside the consultation desk showing views of the Eiffel Tower.

A surname doing commercial work

The store follows the brand's Dorée collection in its design, with elongated lines, structural forms and an open, gallery-like layout, which is retail language for removing the case-and-counter transaction and replacing it with a consultation.

Dousset describes the proposition as pairing lab-grown stones with "exceptional design and uncompromising craftsmanship," which is the standard positioning claim in this category and is not by itself evidence of anything. What is evidence is where the money went.

A brand with three stores put its third in a mall whose catchment earns its income from technology.

What the address is arguing

Price points are not disclosed, which limits what can be concluded from any of it. What can be said is that the expansion is happening while the one-carat lab-grown reference this paper's tape carries fell again today, to $703, and while Chinese lab-grown exports to industrial buyers rose 65.3% in the first half.

Those three facts together describe a category whose input price keeps falling, whose industrial demand is climbing, and whose retail end is being built out by brands selling design rather than carat weight. A retailer competing on stone price is fully exposed to the first fact.

A retailer competing on a name and a gallery is not.
