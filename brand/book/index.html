The symbol

A diamond that is also a pen.

Printers set type in points. Dealers weigh diamonds in points. One point is a hundredth of a carat, and the mark sits where the two trades meet.

  1. 01

    The stone.

    A round brilliant seen from the side. The crown is cut at 34.5°, the angle Marcel Tolkowsky published in Diamond Design in 1919.

  2. 02

    The pen.

    Its pavilion is a nib. The slit runs down the centre facet, and the two pavilion mains become the tines.

  3. 03

    The point.

    The nib's breather hole holds a red point. The same point closes the name.

  4. 04

    The Point.

    Every line is a facet edge or the slit of a nib. Nothing is there for decoration, so it holds together at every size.

The pavilion runs deeper than an ideal cut, 45° against 40.75°. That extra depth is what lets the stone read as a nib.

Optical sizes

Three cuts for three distances.

Fine knockouts disappear on small screens. So the mark comes in three cuts. Each keeps the silhouette and the point, and drops detail as it shrinks.

L cut

96 px and up · 25 mm and up

All facets. The master drawing.

M cut

32–96 px · 9–25 mm

Crown facets removed, knockouts doubled.

S cut

16–32 px · under 9 mm

Girdle, slit and point only. Favicons and app bars.

The engraved cut

For print that invites a closer look.

Each facet is hatched at its own angle and density, lit from the upper left, so the stone reads in pure line.

Colour

Black, Pearl White, and the point.

Two colours carry the brand. Black for the mark and the type, Pearl White for the ground. Point Red is kept for the point, and never becomes a background for long text.

Click a swatch to copy its hex. ¹ CMYK values are converted from RGB as a starting point. Ask the printer for a proof, and match it to these swatches.

Six approved pairings.

The point stays red in every pairing but one: one-colour work, where it takes the colour of the mark. On a photograph the mark always sits on a plate.

Voice

Calm, exact, a little dry.

We write like the best-informed person in the room. No need to raise our voice: no hype words, no exclamation marks, and every headline a promise the article keeps.

  1. 01

    Original reporting only.

  2. 02

    Every claim priced, sourced or cut.

  3. 03

    One quote at most, under fifteen words, attributed.

  4. 04

    No hype words. No exclamation marks.

  5. 05

    Never fabricate a price. Verify it, or carry it as “unch.”

  6. 06

    A short honest edition beats a padded one.

The promise test · headline lab, 21 October 2026

A headline that could mislead is killed, whatever it would score.

The house line

Clarity, daily.

Always with the comma and the full stop. Never italic, never quoted, never punned.

Imagery

Lit once.

Limelight lit the nineteenth-century stage. One hard source, a painted backdrop, and everything else falling to black. Every still here is made that way, one for each desk.

Key · one hard source The stone · brightest point Painted canvas ground Falloff to black

How a still is lit.

The light

One hard key, high and to one side. No fill card. The shadow side is allowed to go.

The ground

Hand-painted canvas in umber, slate or teal. Never a paper sweep, never a gradient.

The subject

A stone in a hand, on velvet or under a loupe. People appear as dealers, cutters and the wearers.

The grain

Fine analogue grain. No sharpening halo, no glow added afterwards.

× Never

Text, logos or real brands inside the picture. The mark set straight onto a still. A generated still beside a news story.
