Diamonds

GIA will comment on fluorescence on about 3% of reports

A named spokesman confirms a fourth-quarter announcement before two new comments reach grading reports. Roughly 30% of submitted stones glow enough to be noteworthy, and about a tenth of those glow strongly.

By Carat Capital

18 October 2026

What changed

The report will say the glow is not a fault. GIA is preparing to add fluorescence comments to diamond grading reports and plans a fourth-quarter announcement first, its director of corporate communications told JCK. Two comments are expected, one flattering and one cautionary.

What it means

  • If you sell fluorescent goods: The flattering comment is expected on the large majority of stones that get one, stating fluorescence may enhance appearance in ultraviolet-rich light such as daylight.
  • If you buy on the discount: The discount has been priced on a trait the lab says is neutral to positive in most stones. A report line removes the argument from the seller's word.
  • What this is not: A change to the grading scale or to any price list. No fluorescence grade changes, and neither GIA nor the report says what a comment does to value.

An announcement comes first

Q4 is the window. GIA plans an announcement in the fourth quarter to inform clients and the trade of the details before the comments are applied to new reports.

A named officer confirms. Stephen Morisseau, GIA's director of corporate communications, gave the plan to JCK, confirming details first reported elsewhere.

Wording not final. The report states GIA's final wording is still being worked out and describes both comments as expected rather than issued.

Two comments, very different sizes

The flattering one dominates. The expected comment states fluorescence may enhance a diamond's appearance in environments rich in ultraviolet light, such as natural daylight.

The warning is rare. A second comment is expected on under 0.2% of natural diamonds GIA certifies. It says fluorescence can make reduced transparency, haziness or milkiness more noticeable under ultraviolet-rich lighting.

Research drives it. Asked what prompted the change, Morisseau pointed to the institute's published research. He said it shows a 'neutral-to-positive effect on appearance' in general.

The discount has a beneficiary

One designer benefits. Gemologist and designer Erica Silverglide built her Ignite collection around fluorescent stones, hand-sorting accent diamonds under ultraviolet light and keeping the medium and strong ones.

Both sides argue. Many in the trade benefit from the disagreement depending on whether they are bidding or asking. Dealers use ultraviolet light to talk prices down, the report notes.

A 1980s label persists. Silverglide recalls a 1980s magazine article dismissing fluorescent diamonds as a poor man's stone, a characterisation the report says has been massively criticised since.

What to watch

  • Q4 2026GIA's own announcement, which the institute says comes before any comment appears on a report.
  • After rolloutThe final wording of both comments. The report calls the current text expected, not issued.
  • OngoingWhether published fluorescence discounts narrow. No price list has changed on this news.

The story so far

  1. 27 July 2026From the fourth quarter GIA will add clarifying language separating fluorescence that changes a diamond's look from fluorescence that does not, on a trait carried by between 25% and 35% of diamonds.

Go deeper

What would change this call

GIA's own announcement saying something else. Every share here is the source's, attributed to GIA and to a 2021 peer-reviewed paper, and the comment wording is explicitly described as not final. If the institute's fourth-quarter announcement sets a different threshold - a different fluorescence level, or a comment on all graded stones rather than the strongly fluorescent minority - the roughly 3% in this headline is wrong. This paper has not read GIA's own document, because it does not yet exist.

What fluorescence is, and why a report line is a price event

Fluorescence is the soft glow a diamond gives off under ultraviolet light, usually blue, caused by nitrogen in the stone's lattice. It is not a flaw in the sense a feather or a crystal inclusion is a flaw, and GIA has graded it for decades on a descriptive scale running from none to very strong rather than on a quality scale. The trade nevertheless priced it as a defect. Rapaport has published fluorescence discount estimates since 1993, and the discount has persisted through every swing of the market, which is a strong clue that it was a convention rather than a measurement. The reason a comment matters is that a grading report is the only document a buyer and a seller both accept. Everything else in a diamond transaction is argument. A dealer with an ultraviolet lamp can say a stone looks hazy, and a seller without a document to point at has to accept the price that follows. A line on the report that states fluorescence may improve the stone's appearance in daylight moves the burden of proof: the discount now has to be argued against the laboratory rather than merely asserted over the counter. That is also why the small number matters more than the large one here. If the comment landed on a third of the market it would be a repricing; landing on roughly 3%, it is a precedent, and the precedent is that the lab will say in writing when the trait is harmless.
