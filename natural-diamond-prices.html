The Natural Diamond Price ListEvery shape, every weight, trade and retail side by side

What a natural diamond actually costs — the wholesale benchmark the trade pays, the asking price the counter shows, and the spread between them, across ten shapes and eleven weight bands. Every figure is dated, every source is named, and every number we modelled rather than observed says so.

The benchmark stone — 1.00 ct round brilliant, G colour, VS1 clarity, GIA

The counter price is 84% above the trade benchmark and still about 35% below Rapaport list. Both things are true at once: the retail margin is real, and the list itself has stopped being the price anyone pays.

A note on this figure

The 4.00–4.99 ct band is the one figure on this page we decline to print. IDEX carries it at $32,660 a carat — above the five-carat band at $21,099 — on 0.32% of the market. A four-carat stone does not cost more per carat than a five-carat stone. The band is too thin to mean anything, so it stays blank rather than being smoothed into something that looks like a price.

A note on this figure

No public source publishes a wholesale per-carat figure for ten carats. IDEX carries no driver at that weight, RAPI publishes no level, and the independent Antwerp grids stop at six. The ten-carat line is a live retail asking average and is marked as such.

Two benchmarks — why the trade quotes two numbers

WeightRAPI · D–H / IF–VS2IDEX · market averageGap
0.30 ct$899no driver—
0.50 ct$1,231$1,315+6.8%
1.00 ct$3,880$3,033−21.8%
3.00 ct$17,879$16,375−8.4%

RAPI is the average asking price of the ten per cent lowest-priced D–H, IF–VS2, GIA-graded stones on RapNet. It is a floor for a narrow top specification, not a market mean. IDEX price drivers average a far broader spread of colour and clarity. At one carat the broad market sits 22% below the narrow top spec, which is what you would expect — and a useful reminder that a single 'diamond price' does not exist.

A note on this figure

Pear and marquise are the two shapes we will not price to a single number. Live inventory at one house puts pear 44% below its own round; another retailer's published differential puts it 18.7% above. That is not rounding — it is two different books. Read the shape tier, not the decimal.

The roundbaseline

Roughly a third of rough is lost cutting one. You pay for the waste.

Elongated brilliants−17 to −23%

Oval, pear, marquise. Cut with the grain of the rough, so less is thrown away — and they look larger face-up than their weight.

Step cuts and squares−33 to −43%

Emerald, asscher, radiant, princess, cushion, heart. Cheapest per carat, and the least forgiving: an open table shows every inclusion the round would have hidden.

A note on this figure

SI2 costs 59.6% less than IF. The two grades that matter most to a buyer's wallet are the ones nobody can see: the step from VS2 to SI1 costs 17% and is invisible to the eye in most stones.

A note on this figure

We publish colour and clarity as two separate ladders, never crossed into a grid. Multiplying one by the other would reconstruct a proprietary price list cell by cell, and that list is somebody else's copyrighted product. The ladders are drawn from published index aggregates; the grid is not ours to print.

The magic weights — what crossing a round number costs

StepPer caratReading
0.90 → 1.00 ct+23 to +24%Like for like, same colour and clarity. Four independent methods converge inside a single point.
0.30 → 0.50 ct+37%Per carat, RAPI against RAPI.

A note on this figure

Divide the 1.00 ct row of the main table by the 0.90 ct row and you get +60%, not +24%. Both are right and they measure different things. The main table averages every stone inside a weight band, and the specification mix changes as the bands rise — more of the better colours sit above a carat. The +23 to +24% here is the same colour and the same clarity on both sides of the line. That figure is the one to plan a purchase around.

Buy 0.90 and nobody at the table can tell. That is the single cheapest piece of advice on this page.

A note on this figure

The fancy-shape discount has inverted in lab-grown. Where a natural oval costs 23% less than a natural round, a lab oval costs 11% more than a lab round — and a lab princess costs 23% more. In a market with no scarcity, price follows growing time and cutting loss rather than rarity, and the ranking flips. It is the cleanest single illustration of what the two markets actually are.

What moved the numbers — the market behind the table

M—01

Rough is still falling

De Beers realised $110 a carat in the second quarter of 2026, down 37% year on year; $105 across the half, down 32%. Its half-year price index reads 69, off 16%. The full-year 2025 average was $142.

M—02

Polished has stopped falling at the bottom

RAPI at 0.30 ct is up 7.7% so far this year even as it sits 23.3% below last July. The small-stone recovery is the first sustained upward move in the index in two years.

M—03

The middle is still soft

One carat is down 6.4% year to date and 15.3% year on year. Three carat is close to flat: −1.8% and −0.8%.

M—04

Tariffs arrived in July

US duty effective 24 July 2026: India 10%, EU zero, Hong Kong and China 12.5% plus Section 301. Canada goes to 50% from 19 August.

M—05

Sanctions bite at half a carat

The G7 threshold on Russian-origin stones has stood at 0.50 ct since 1 September 2024, and a due-diligence statement has been required on polished imports since 1 January 2026.

M—06

Fancy colour is flat, not falling

The Fancy Colour Research Foundation index was −0.2% on the quarter and −0.9% on the year in Q1 2026. Blue is best read as unchanged: two houses reported the same release as +0.3% and −0.3%.

M—07

The top of the market is fine

Gem Diamonds averaged $1,395 a carat in the first half, up 38%, with a 52.24 ct white at $32,908 a carat. At auction the Ocean Dream, 5.5 ct vivid blue-green, made $17.3m — about $3.15m a carat.

M—08

And De Beers is changing hands

The Gareth Penny consortium is reported at about $1bn for Anglo American's 85%, against a book value near $2.3bn earlier this year.

Method — observed, modelled, withheld

What is observed

The trade per-carat column at 0.50, 0.70, 0.90, 1.00, 1.50, 2.00, 3.00 and 5.00 carats is read directly from IDEX price drivers on 30 July 2026. The 0.30 ct figure is RAPI at 1 July 2026. The one-carat retail figure is the median of six retailers priced on 30 July 2026. The shape table is a live sample of 169,324 asking prices. The colour and clarity indices are published aggregates at 31 December 2025.

What is modelled

Retail per carat at every weight other than one carat. It is the trade benchmark multiplied by a factor that falls from 2.10 at 0.30 ct to 1.35 at 5.00 ct, calibrated on the observed 1.84 at one carat and cross-checked against the disclosed gross margins of three listed retailers. Rows carrying modelled figures say so in the table.

What is withheld

The 4.00–4.99 ct band, because its published per-carat average exceeds the band above it on a sample of 0.32%. And the colour × clarity grid, because building one from published premiums would reconstruct a copyrighted price list.

Units

All figures are US dollars per carat unless a whole-stone column says otherwise. Rapaport's index documentation describes its levels in hundreds of dollars per carat; at that reading a one-carat stone would price near $388,000. The levels are only coherent as plain dollars per carat and are printed here as such.

What this is not

This is a market reference, not a valuation and not an offer. Every stone prices on its own certificate, its own cut and its own day.
