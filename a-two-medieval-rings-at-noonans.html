Noonans lists two medieval gold rings at £9,000 to £13,000

Andrew Riley found both with a metal detector in fields near Northallerton in 2024, four months apart. The Blood of Christ ring carries £5,000 to £7,000 and the St Margaret ring £4,000 to £6,000.

By The Auctions Desk19 September 2026Brief 25 sec · Full read 1 min
What changed

Two rings reach the room. Noonans Mayfair sells two 15th-century gold rings on 22 September 2026, carrying published bands of £5,000 to £7,000 and £4,000 to £6,000.

What it means · The Desk’s View

If you buy medieval gold: two rings from one finder and one district come up in a single session.

If you estimate devotional jewellery: the enamelled ring carries the higher band by £1,000 at each end.

What this is not: a result. Nothing has sold, and both figures are the house's own estimates.

IOne finder, two rings, four months

Riley found both. Andrew Riley, a semi-retired architect from North Yorkshire, found the rings in fields near Northallerton in 2024.

Eight inches down. The first lay about eight inches deep in a ridge and furrow field he had searched for weeks.

IIEstimates are published, results are not

Bands, not prices. The house has published both bands. Nothing is sold until 22 September 2026, so no result exists to report.

The enamel carries more. The blood of Christ ring is banded £1,000 higher than the St Margaret ring at each end.

IIIProvenance research is open

A guild connection is unproven. JCK notes the Corpus Christi Guild at York, founded in 1408, came to number 17,000 members.

One member lived near. A landowner in that guild held land close to the findspot, a link JCK itself calls possibly coincidental.

01What would change this call+

A hammer price outside either band. An estimate is the house's public expectation, and a two-ring consignment with a story attached is exactly the kind that clears it or misses it widely.

02How a treasure-find consignment reaches a saleroom+

In England and Wales a find of this age and metal is reported to a coroner and assessed under the Treasure Act. If no museum acquires it, the object returns to the finder and landowner, who by convention split the proceeds. That is why a metal-detector find can arrive at a commercial saleroom two years after it leaves the ground, and why the finder is named in the catalogue rather than a dealer.
