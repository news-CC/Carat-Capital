Valcambi signs a Hong Kong refining venture with Shiu Wing

The letter of intent covers a refining and vaulting plant at Shiu Wing Steel's Tuen Mun site. No investment figure, capacity or timetable is published. Hong Kong's finance secretary was at the signing.

By The Bullion Desk

12 October 2026

What changed

A letter, not a deal. Valcambi and Shiu Wing Steel signed a letter of intent on 7 October to form a Hong Kong refining company. No terms were given.

What it means

  • If you refine or vault in Asia: A Swiss refiner is proposing capacity inside Hong Kong rather than shipping metal to Switzerland.
  • If you trade Hong Kong bullion: The government was in the room. The finance secretary arrived from the LBMA conference in Sorrento.
  • What this is not: A signed joint venture, a funded project, or a plant with a date. A letter of intent commits neither party to build.

A letter signed in Balerna

Two companies, one plan. Valcambi and Shiu Wing Steel aim to establish a new enterprise headquartered in Hong Kong. The release calls the instrument a letter of intent.

At Tuen Mun. The project would put a refining and vaulting operation at Shiu Wing's existing facility in Tuen Mun. No build date is given.

A government guest. Christopher Hui, Hong Kong's Secretary for Financial Services and the Treasury, visited the Balerna plant on 7 October from the LBMA conference in Sorrento.

A steel mill diversifies

Founded in 1956. Shiu Wing Steel was founded by the late Pong Ding Yuen and is Hong Kong's only steel mill. It has made and sold rebar for over 65 years.

Landmarks, not bullion. The release names the Mass Transit Railway, the Cross-Harbour Tunnels, Hong Kong International Airport, Jardine House, Taikoo Shing and the IFC among its projects.

A new chapter. Dario Pong, the mill's executive director, frames refining as industrial diversification. Simone Knobloch, Valcambi's chief executive, puts the reasoning on the region: "Asia is a key market for the precious metals industry."

Nothing is committed yet

No figure anywhere. The release gives no investment sum, no refining capacity and no completion date. The parties intend to evaluate creating a refining platform.

Clearing was present. Ping Keung Chan, chief executive of Hong Kong Precious Metals Central Clearing Company, appears in the release's own photograph caption.

What to watch

What would change this call

A definitive agreement, or silence. A letter of intent is not a contract and either side may walk; Valcambi's own wording is that the parties intend to evaluate a platform. An investment sum, a tonnage or a commissioning date would each turn this from an intention into a project, and none has been published. A refusal of a Hong Kong site permit, or a definitive agreement naming a different site, would change the reading entirely.

Why a refiner would want metal refined in Hong Kong

Refining capacity is not evenly spread. Switzerland refines a disproportionate share of the world's gold, which means metal mined or scrapped in Asia has historically travelled to Europe, been refined to good-delivery standard, and travelled back to the Asian markets that bought it. Every leg of that journey costs freight, insurance and time, and every leg is a point at which a bar leaves one jurisdiction's chain of custody for another's. A plant in Tuen Mun shortens the loop: metal refined in Hong Kong can be vaulted in Hong Kong and delivered against Hong Kong contracts without crossing a continent twice. That is also why the clearing company's chief executive is in the photograph. A refinery is only useful to a market if the bars it makes are deliverable into that market's own clearing system, and Hong Kong has set itself a first-quarter 2027 target for central gold clearing. The territory has been assembling the pieces of a bullion centre in public: a clearing house, a stated government plan, and now a letter of intent for the plant that would make the metal. What it does not have yet is a number attached to any of it.
