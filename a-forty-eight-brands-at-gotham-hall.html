Watches

48 brands fill Gotham Hall for New York Watch Week

WatchTime New York runs 16 to 18 October at its eleventh edition, with the Windup Watch Fair on the same dates. Friday night is sold out. No sales figure is published.

By The Watch Desk

12 October 2026

What changed

The exhibitor count is 48. WatchTime New York will show 48 brands at Gotham Hall from 16 to 18 October, which organisers call a record for the eleven-year-old event.

What it means

  • If you retail watches in New York: Four events run across four days, and two of them are already fully booked or sold out in part.
  • If you track independent brands: The exhibitor list is weighted to independents, some of which organisers say carry delivery waits beyond five years.
  • What this is not: A sales event with published numbers, a trade-only fair, or a figure anyone has audited. The counts are the organisers' own and the week has not happened.

Gotham Hall takes forty-eight

A counted list. JCK names every confirmed exhibitor and the list runs to 48. Organisers call it a new high for the show's eleven years.

Independents dominate. Armin Strom, Czapek, H. Moser & Cie., Laurent Ferrier, MB&F, Urwerk and Voutilainen appear alongside Chopard, Citizen, Piaget and Zenith.

Premieres are promised. Blancpain will show a new Lady B. Organisers confirmed that Kudoke and Frederique Constant are debuting models at the show.

Four events, four days

Two on Thursday. The FHH New York Watch Summit runs 15 October at Neuehouse Madison Square with four panels. Indies.NYC runs the same day and is fully booked.

Two from Friday. WatchTime New York and the Windup Watch Fair both run 16 to 18 October, at Gotham Hall and Center415 respectively.

One panel asks. The FHH panels cover knowledge, what qualifies as an icon, perceived value, and whether America keeps its place as the largest fine-watch market.

No figure is published

Tickets, not revenue. The only demand signal is ticket supply. Friday night is sold out and single days remain for Saturday and Sunday.

Waits beyond five years. Sara Orlando, WatchTime's publisher, says of the independents taking part: "Many of the brands have wait lists exceeding five years for delivery."

What to watch

  • After the showWhether WatchTime publishes an attendance number. It published none for this edition in advance.
  • OngoingWhether the United States holds its place as the largest market for fine watch sales, the question FHH has put on a panel.

What would change this call

An attendance or sales figure, which would turn a calendar into a market reading. None exists for any of the four events and none has been published for this show in previous years. The 48 is an exhibitor count, not a measure of trade: a show can add brands in a falling market, and stands are sold months ahead of the week they fill. A cancellation, or a brand withdrawing between now and 16 October, would move the only number this piece carries.

Why exhibitor counts are the only public series

New York Watch Week is not one event and nobody owns it. It is a cluster of independently run shows, summits and brand showings that have gravitated to the same four days in October, and that structure is why there is no number for it. A trade fair with a single organiser publishes attendance because exhibitors pay for stands on the strength of it. A loose week of consumer-facing shows, private appointments and panel discussions has no single party with an interest in counting, and no audited gate figure has ever been published for WatchTime New York. What is left is the exhibitor count, which is public because the list of who is coming is itself the marketing. That makes it a supply number rather than a demand number: it says how many brands bought space, not how many watches moved or how many people came. It is still worth printing, because it is countable and because it can be checked against the published list, which is more than can be said for the adjectives around it. But a reader should treat a rising exhibitor count as evidence about the cost and perceived value of exhibiting, not as evidence about watch sales in America.
