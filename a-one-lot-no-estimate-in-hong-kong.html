One lot, no estimate: Christie’s offers a Qianlong ewer

The solid-gold ewer is set with pearls, rubies, sapphires and turquoise and carries a Qianlong mark. It comes from Dr Carl Kempe's collection and sells in Hong Kong on 29 October.

By The Auction Desk

11 October 2026

What changed

One object, one sale. Christie's has given a Qianlong imperial gold ewer its own single-lot auction in Hong Kong on 29 October. The release names no estimate and no reserve.

What it means

  • If you set stones in gold: Filigree and four stone types are what this catalogue will be judged on.
  • If you plan to bid: No estimate exists to anchor against. The preview is the only price discovery.
  • What this is not: A result, a record, or a published estimate.

A single lot, alone

One object, one sale. The release calls it a dedicated single-lot auction during Hong Kong Asian Art Week. Christie's describes the ewer as among the finest surviving imperial gold vessels in private hands.

No estimate, no reserve. Neither figure appears in the release. For a buyer that removes the usual anchor and makes the two previews the only chance to price the object before the room opens.

Gold, filigree, four stones

Solid gold, set. The ewer and cover are fashioned in solid gold, as the release describes them. They are embellished with pearls, rubies, sapphires and turquoise over intricate Imperial Workshop filigree.

A Qianlong mark. It carries a Qianlong mark and is of the period, given as 1736 to 1795. Christie's compares the ornament to the Jin'ou Yonggu cup used by Qing emperors at New Year.

Seventeen forty-one. Imperial Workshop records describe a jewel-embellished gold ewer commissioned in 1740 and completed in 1741. The release says it corresponds to this work in scale and decoration.

Kempe owned it

A named collection. The ewer was in the collection of Dr Carl Kempe, 1884 to 1967. The release calls him one of the foremost European collectors of Chinese art of his century.

Exhibited and published. Christie's states the piece has been exhibited internationally and published extensively. For a single-lot sale with no estimate, that record is the only signal of confidence on offer.

What to watch

  • Before 17 OctoberWhether an estimate is published with the catalogue. None exists in the announcement release.
  • 17–28 OctoberThe two previews, in Taipei and Hong Kong. Pre-registration is required and venues may change.
  • 29 OctoberThe result. A single-lot sale has no other lots to cushion it, so the hammer is the whole outcome.

What would change this call

A published estimate, which would turn this from an unpriced event into a priced one and is the single most useful thing that could appear before 17 October. The reading would also change if the correspondence between the 1740–41 workshop record and this ewer is contested: the release says the archival entry corresponds in scale and decoration, which is a careful phrase and not a claim that this is the recorded object.

What a single-lot sale is for

A house normally sells an object inside a session, where thirty or sixty lots share the audience, the marketing and the risk. A single-lot sale strips all of that away and spends a whole saleroom slot, a whole press cycle and a whole evening's attention on one thing. That is expensive, which is why houses reserve the format for objects they believe will carry it, and it is why the format itself is read as a signal in the absence of a number. The trade consequence is that the usual comparables disappear. In a mixed session a bidder can see what the lot before and after did, and an underbid is partly absorbed by the session total. Here there is no session total, no neighbouring lot and, as announced, no estimate: the hammer is the entire public record the sale will produce. For anyone valuing comparable Qing imperial gold afterwards, that makes the 29 October figure unusually load-bearing, whichever way it goes.
