Diamonds

Richemont and Signet are in talks to join the De Beers bid

Bloomberg and Reuters report both in talks to join the Global Diamond Consortium, the preferred bidder for Anglo American's De Beers stake. Neither investment would necessarily be large.

By Carat Capital

18 October 2026

What changed

Two buyers of diamonds may become owners of them. Richemont and Signet are in talks to join the Global Diamond Consortium, the preferred bidder for Anglo American's De Beers stake. Bloomberg and Reuters report the talks. Neither investment would necessarily be large.

What it means

  • If you sell rough into the sightholder system: The consortium would gain two of the largest buyers of finished natural goods. An owner who also retails has a reason to defend the natural category that a financial owner does not.
  • If you compete with the consortium: The bid is widening rather than narrowing. Namibia, Angola and Botswana are reported as holders alongside private investors already committed.
  • What this is not: A completed investment. The report says there is no guarantee either company participates, and names no sum for either.

Two retailers, one bid

The talks are reported. Richemont and Signet are discussing joining the Global Diamond Consortium, according to Bloomberg and Reuters as carried by JCK. There is no guarantee either takes part.

Richemont is the weight. The Swiss group owns Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels, and the report puts jewellery at about three-quarters of its business. It gives no revenue figure.

Signet is the volume. Signet owns Kay Jewelers, Zales and Jared in the United States. The report names it one of the world's largest jewellery retailers and attaches no sales figure.

Why a retailer owner matters

The category wants advocates. JCK notes the diamond industry has long sought stronger support from luxury and jewellery retailers in promoting natural stones against lab-grown competition.

An analyst's framing. An RBC Capital Markets analyst told Reuters that Richemont is a major user of De Beers diamonds. That makes it an important voice in the industry's future.

Size is secondary. The report states neither proposed investment would necessarily be large. Influence inside the consortium is the thing being bought, not a controlling share.

The states stay short of control

Three producers, under half. Botswana, Namibia and Angola are expected to hold significant stakes and together to hold less than 50% of De Beers, leaving control in private hands.

Private money is committed. Several private investors are already committed to the consortium, which the report says is headed by former De Beers chief executive Gareth Penny.

July set the frame. The consortium emerged in July as Anglo American's preferred bidder, reported here at the time. The question since has been who sits inside it.

What to watch

  • Q4 2026Whether either company confirms participation. Both are reported in talks and neither has announced anything.
  • OngoingThe size of Botswana's stake, reported separately as nearing agreement at about a quarter of the company.
  • At signingWhether the producing states' combined holding is published. The report gives only a bound of less than half.

The story so far

  1. 30 July 2026The Global Diamond Consortium's offer for De Beers was reported at about $1 billion with $750 million up front.
  2. 20 July 2026Botswana's minister told parliament the state's 15% of De Beers carries a right of first refusal and complete freedom in how it is used.

What would change this call

A denial, or a confirmation with a number. Everything here rests on Bloomberg's and Reuters' sourcing as carried by JCK; neither Richemont nor Signet has said anything on the record, and the report itself says participation is not guaranteed. If either company states it is not in talks, this story is wrong in its central claim. If either confirms with a sum, the framing changes from influence to capital and the article understates it.

What a preferred bidder is, and what it is not

Anglo American named the Global Diamond Consortium its preferred bidder in July 2026. Preferred-bidder status in a disposal of this kind is an exclusivity arrangement, not a sale: the seller agrees to negotiate with one party while the party assembles its money, its partners and its regulatory clearances. The seller can still walk, and the bidder's composition can change until the agreement is signed, which is precisely why two new names appearing in talks three months later is news rather than noise. Botswana's position is a separate instrument again. The state's 15% carries a right of first refusal, confirmed to Botswana's parliament in July and reported by this paper at the time, which means Gaborone can match an offer rather than merely object to one. A consortium that wants certainty therefore has two problems to solve at once: find enough capital, and settle with the one shareholder who can pre-empt it. Adding retailers addresses neither problem directly. What it addresses is demand. De Beers' difficulty is not that nobody will buy the company, it is that natural diamond demand has been eroded by lab-grown goods, and an owner who also runs Cartier or Kay has a commercial reason to market the natural stone that an investment fund does not.
