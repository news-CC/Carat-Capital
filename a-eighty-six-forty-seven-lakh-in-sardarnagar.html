₹86.47 lakh of gold and silver never reach a Sardarnagar buyer

A woman's Ahmedabad complaint itemises ₹23.47 lakh for 150 grams of gold and ₹63 lakh for 28 kilos of silver, neither delivered. Police tell Divya Bhaskar more than twenty people dealt with the pair over seven months.

By Carat Capital

17 October 2026

What changed

A complaint names a figure. Ahmedabad's crime branch has registered a ₹86.47 lakh case against the owners of Komal Jewellers in Sardarnagar. The complaint itemises 150 grams of gold and 28 kilos of silver, paid for and not delivered.

What it means

  • If you buy gold outside the bill: The two line items here price gold at ₹15,646.67 a gram. That is above the full, undiscounted rate a Bhuj seller named six days earlier.
  • If you run a counter in Gujarat: This is the third alleged jewellery fraud in the state this paper has reported in a week, and the second in Ahmedabad.
  • What this is not: A conviction, a charge sheet or an assay. No metal has been tested, no court has ruled, and the ₹100 crore aggregate is a police estimate given to a newspaper.

Two line items and a total

The woman's figures. She told the crime branch she paid ₹23.47 lakh towards 150 grams of gold and ₹63 lakh towards 28 kilos of silver. Neither was delivered.

They add up exactly. The two amounts sum to ₹86.47 lakh, the figure the complaint states. Police registered the case at a rounded ₹87 lakh.

The pair are absent. Divya Bhaskar reports the father and son as having fled. No charge sheet has been filed and no court has heard the matter.

The discount that was not one

The pitch was Dubai. The pair told their community the gold came cheap out of Dubai. They offered a tola at ₹5,000 to ₹6,000 under market, and silver up to ₹10,000 less a kilo.

The maths disagrees. Her gold works out at ₹15,646.67 a gram. A Bhuj agent this paper reported on 11 October called ₹14,000 a gram the full, undiscounted rate for 24-carat metal.

Purity is the gap. Bhuj was quoted as 24 carat. The Ahmedabad complaint states no fineness, no hallmark and no date for either transaction, so the two are not like metal.

The bigger number is softer

Police estimate the rest. Officers put the combined value ordered over seven months at more than ₹100 crore. Individual dealings ran ₹50 lakh to ₹1 crore.

It carries no document. That aggregate is a round figure given to a reporter. No charge sheet, seizure memo or itemised schedule is cited anywhere in the report.

Most deliveries happened. Police describe metal changing hands for most of the seven months, with buyers reselling at a profit. The money disappeared on the final lot.

What to watch

  • Next few weeksA charge sheet. It would replace the ₹100 crore estimate with an itemised total and name the number of complainants.
  • OngoingWhether either man is arrested. Both are reported absconding and neither has answered the allegation.
  • OngoingWhether any seized metal is assayed. No fineness has been established for anything described in this case.

Go deeper

What would change this call

An assay and a charge sheet. Every figure here comes from one complainant's statement as a newspaper reports it, and a first information report records what a complainant says and nothing more. If the 150 grams were to include making charges, stones or a part-payment against a larger order, the per-gram figure collapses and the comparison with Bhuj goes with it. The report does not say, and this paper has not reached the complainant or the crime branch.

Why a fake-smuggling pitch needs a real delivery first

The structure in this complaint is older than the metal. A seller offers goods below market on a premise the buyer cannot verify and would not want verified, usually that duty has been avoided. The illegality is the point: it explains the discount, it explains the absence of a bill, and it guarantees the buyer will not complain to anyone while the arrangement is working. The first several rounds are then delivered honestly, at or near the promised price, which is what the police account describes here across most of seven months. Those rounds are not the fraud, they are the cost of it. They convert a stranger into a repeat customer who tells others, which is how twenty people from one community end up dealing with the same two men. The loss arrives on the largest lot, after the buyer has scaled up on the strength of the earlier ones, and it arrives at a moment when the victim has the weakest possible footing for a complaint. What makes this particular case worth printing is that the arithmetic in the one itemised complaint does not support the premise at all. The woman paid more per gram than a different Gujarat seller called his full rate six days earlier, in this paper's own reporting. The discount was the story the buyers were told. It was not, on these two numbers, the deal they got.
