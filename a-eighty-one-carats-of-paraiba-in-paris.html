Colored Gemstones

81.17-carat Paraiba leads Bonhams Paris on 29 October

The neon-blue cushion-cut tourmaline carries a €60,000 to €80,000 estimate. Two unheated sapphires over 10 carats, signed Cartier and Chaumet, follow at €40,000 to €60,000 and €45,000 to €65,000.

By The Coloured Stones Desk

10 October 2026

What changed

Paris gets a date and a lead stone. Bonhams will sell Fine Jewellery in Paris on 29 October. An 81.17-carat neon-blue Paraiba tourmaline leads at a €60,000 low estimate.

What it means

  • If you buy coloured stones at auction: Three of the four lead stones are described as untreated. Read each report.
  • If you consign tourmaline: Eighty-one carats is being asked to clear what a 12-carat sapphire clears.
  • What this is not: A result, or a trophy sale. The top estimate is €80,000.

One stone carries the sale

Eighty-one carats. The lead lot is a Kat Florence pendant necklace on a cushion-cut natural Paraiba tourmaline of 81.17 carats. Its colour is neon blue and the mount is openwork brilliant-cut diamond.

Sixty to eighty. Bonhams estimates it at €60,000 to €80,000. That is the highest estimate in the catalogue the house has published.

Seven hundred a carat. Attributing the whole low estimate to the stone gives €739 a carat. That is an upper bound on the implied stone price, not a stone price.

Untreated is the term

Chaumet, 12.5 carats. An octagonal step-cut Sri Lankan sapphire of 12.5 carats, with no indications of heating, is offered at €45,000 to €65,000 in a Chaumet Paris ring.

Cartier, 11.5 carats. An oval Royal Blue sapphire of Madagascan origin, 11.5 carats, with no indications of treatment, carries €40,000 to €60,000.

Twenty-two, colour-change. A necklace pendant holds an approximately 22-carat Sri Lankan oval that changes blue to violet, untreated, at €16,000 to €26,000.

The rest is signature

Onyx twice over. An onyx and diamond ring carries €35,000 to €55,000. An onyx, sapphire and diamond bracelet follows it in the table, both on geometric Deco construction.

Five names listed. Buccellati, Lacloche, Schlumberger, Zolotas and Lalaounis are named, with a separate section of artist jewellery. No estimates were published for those.

What to watch

  • 29 October 2026The sale itself, at Avenue Hoche in Paris. Whether the 81.17-carat tourmaline clears €60,000 is the one number that settles this.
  • Before the saleWhether Bonhams publishes the laboratory reports behind the three no-treatment statements. The estimates rest on them and the press release names no laboratory.
  • 10 November 2026Christie's Geneva Magnificent Jewels, the next dated coloured-stone and coloured-diamond test on the autumn calendar.

What would change this call

A published laboratory report that qualifies any of the three no-treatment statements, which would move the estimates rather than the description. It would also read differently if Bonhams adds a trophy lot before the sale: the catalogue as announced tops out at €80,000, and one late consignment at a higher level would change what this sale is for.

Why Paraiba is priced per stone, not per carat

Paraiba tourmaline trades on colour and on copper content, and its auction record is short. The material was only identified in Brazil in the 1980s and the Mozambican and Nigerian sources that supply most of the large stones arrived later still, so there is no century of comparable results to calibrate against in the way there is for Burmese or Ceylon sapphire. That has two consequences for a reader looking at an estimate. The first is that very large Paraiba is not simply small Paraiba multiplied: a stone this size is a distinct object with its own tiny comparable set, and a house pricing it is choosing a number from a handful of prior sales rather than reading it off a curve. The second is that the mount matters more than usual. A signed pendant on an openwork diamond mount carries value that is not in the tourmaline, and no auction house breaks a lot estimate into stone and setting, so any per-carat figure a reader computes from a published estimate is necessarily an upper bound. Sapphire is the opposite case. A 12.5-carat unheated Ceylon stone sits in a deep, long-documented market where heating status is the single largest value determinant, which is why the treatment line in the catalogue does more work than the weight does.
