₹20 lakh advance fee ends a ₹24 crore Surat sale

Mahidharpura police have booked two people who offered to buy a Katargam jeweller's properties for a Dubai company. The money taken was billed as a charge to unfreeze that company's account.

By The Diamond Desk

11 October 2026

What changed

A fee did the work. A Katargam jeweller agreed to sell two properties for ₹24 crore to a buyer claiming a Dubai company. He then paid ₹20 lakh to unfreeze its account, and the buyers vanished.

What it means

  • If you are selling property: The fee was 0.83 percent of the price. That ratio is the fraud's whole design.
  • If you supply on credit: Three of the four complaints are goods released on a promise to pay later.
  • What this is not: A conviction, a recovery, or a figure a court has tested.

The sale was the bait

An advertised property. Gondaliya advertised his home on Sumul Dairy Road and a second property in Paras Society, Katargam. Paresh Yogi of Mumbai answered the advertisement.

A Dubai account. Yogi introduced Rishita Jagdish Patel of Raigad. She said she owned a company and held a 20 percent stake in a Dubai company, which would pay the ₹24 crore direct.

Then a processing charge. The complaint says the account was described as frozen and ₹20 lakh went to Patel's own account to unfreeze it. Both phones were switched off after that.

Three more on trade credit

Ten crore in yarn. Six yarn suppliers at Sachin Hosiery Estate say Bhavin Poly Fab's partners took ₹10.41 crore. More than ₹3.71 crore of yarn moved between 15 November 2025 and 27 May 2026.

Twenty days to pay. The complaint says the buyers promised payment within 20 days, paid small sums early to build trust, then stopped. The economic offences wing now has the file.

A broker and eight. In Salabatpura a saree trader says a cloth broker and eight traders left ₹77.98 lakh unpaid. The goods ran from November 2025 to February 2026, then the shops closed.

What to watch

  • Not announcedWhether Patel and Yogi are arrested. The report says police have begun that process and names no date.
  • Not announcedWhat the economic offences wing finds in the ₹10.41 crore yarn case, the only one of the four handed to a specialist unit.
  • OngoingWhether any of the four amounts is recovered. None is reported recovered and none is adjudicated.

What would change this call

A court. These are first-information reports, which record what a complainant says and nothing more, and every name and amount here could be revised by a charge sheet or dismissed outright. The jewellery relevance would also change if the Mahidharpura complaint turns out to involve the trader's business rather than his personal property: as reported, the ₹24 crore was for two buildings, and no stock, stones or firm assets are named.

Why the small fee is the design

An advance-fee fraud works on a ratio, not an amount. The victim is shown a prize large enough that a payment of under one percent of it reads as administrative friction rather than a loss, and the obstacle invented to justify that payment is always something the victim cannot inspect: a frozen account in another jurisdiction, a clearance, a tax, a release fee. Here the ratio was 0.83 percent, and the uninspectable thing was a bank account in Dubai. The practical test for a trade reader is simple and it does not need a lawyer. Money should never move towards the buyer. In an ordinary property sale the buyer's costs are the buyer's, a frozen account is the buyer's problem to solve before the deal proceeds, and a seller who is asked to fund the other side's paperwork is already in a different transaction from the one advertised. The other three complaints in the same week are the mirror image, where goods rather than cash moved first and the promise to pay was the thing that could not be inspected.
