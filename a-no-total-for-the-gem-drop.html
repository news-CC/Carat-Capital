Two Sotheby's jewel sales closed. Neither has published a number

The Gem Drop August closed in New York on 13 August and the Arcade Sale in Hong Kong on 6 August. Sotheby's own results index carries no total for either, and the July edition still carries none after 32 days.

By The Auctions Desk17 August 2026Brief 60 sec · Full read 4 minCorrected · see The depth
What changed

The Gem Drop August closed online in New York on 13 August after four days of bidding. Four days later Sotheby's own results index lists the sale as past and shows no total, no sell-through rate and no top lot against it.

What it means · The Desk’s View

An auction is a price discovery mechanism, and a price discovery mechanism that does not publish its prices is a shop. That is not a complaint about secrecy; auction houses withhold plenty and are entitled to.

It is a note about what this particular format is being sold as. The premium-free monthly drop is pitched to buyers as the transparent, retail-legible end of the auction business, and it has so far been the least legible thing either house runs, because a retailer at least prints the price on the tag.

This paper's recommendation on 14 August was to retire the Gem Drop result as a story after four days of waiting for it. That was the wrong call and this is the correction to it: the waiting was never the story.

The silence is, and it now has three sales and five weeks behind it.

IThree sales, no numbers

The Arcade Sale in Hong Kong, which ran from 31 July to 6 August, is listed the same way and has been for eleven days. Neither absence is particular to this month.

This desk checked the previous edition of the same series, the Gem Drop July, which closed on 16 July and which this paper covered on 17 July: thirty-two days on, its page carries no result either. Three closed jewellery sales, no published number on any of them.

The series page is the clearest evidence of the pattern. Sotheby's maintains a landing page for the Gem Drop, describing it as a monthly release of jewels through its Sealed platform, and that page carries a heading for past auctions with nothing underneath it.

The lot pages survive, so a reader can still see what was offered and what it was estimated at. What does not survive is what anything sold for, or whether it sold at all.

For a format the house has now run at least seven times on a stated monthly cadence, the accumulated public record of how it has performed is a set of empty listings and an empty heading.

One limit belongs on that finding and is stated rather than buried: Sotheby's builds parts of these pages with scripts this desk's fetch does not execute, so what is reported here is what the public page gives a reader, not proof that no figure exists anywhere inside the house.

IIThe same house, counting elsewhere

The contrast sits inside the same building.

Sotheby's New York High Jewelry sale on 16 June 2026 made $43.4 million with 98% of its 119 lots sold and more than 63% of them above high estimate, led by an unmounted 10.02-carat fancy intense blue diamond at $8.7 million, and the total was in the trade press the following day.

The house reported a $4.4 billion first half for 2026 with private sales at a record $826 million. An auction house that publishes a total for a trophy night inside twenty-four hours, and a half-year figure for its owners, is not a house that cannot count an eleven-lot online sale.

Where the number serves the brand it is published within a day. Where it would answer a question about a new format, it has not been published at all, across three sales and five weeks.

IIIWhat the silence costs the trade

What is lost is a figure the trade has no other route to. The Gem Drop's distinguishing feature is that it charges no buyer's premium, which this desk described on 17 July as the auction house competing with the jewellery store on the store's own terms.

Whether that competition is working is an empirical question with a real answer inside it: hammer against estimate, sell-through, whether the same bidders return each month. Seven editions of that data exist somewhere in Sotheby's and none of it is public.

A dealer weighing a consignment into a no-premium online format, and a retailer wondering whether the format is quietly taking a customer, are both reasoning from the house's own description of itself.

02Corrections1 note

Correction, 18 August 2026: as first published on 17 August this article's second section gave the contrasting sale as "Sotheby's New York High Jewelry sale on 13 June," at $31.4 million with 95% of lots sold and a $3.2 million Graff ring, and said this paper printed those figures on 24 July. Those figures are real but they belong to the sale of 13 June 2025, not to 2026, and they were carried here from this paper's own 24 July article, which had taken them from a source page without checking its publication year. The 2026 sale ran on 16 June 2026 and made $43.4 million with 98% of 119 lots sold, led by a 10.02-carat fancy intense blue diamond at $8.7 million. The corrected figure strengthens rather than weakens the point: the house published a larger total for a bigger sale within a day of the hammer falling. Correct as published were the central finding, that Sotheby's results index carries no total, sell-through or top lot for the Gem Drop August, the Arcade Sale Hong Kong or the Gem Drop July, the day counts of 4, 11 and 32, and the $4.4 billion first half with $826 million in private sales.
