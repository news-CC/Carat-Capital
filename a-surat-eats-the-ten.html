Diamonds

Surat does the math on ten percent

India polishes roughly nine in ten of the world's diamonds by volume, and the United States is its largest customer. A 10% duty on Indian polished, live July 24, lands on a trade already thinned by cheap rough and soft margins.

By The Cutting Desk

25 July 2026

What changed

No country has more exposed to the new tariff schedule than India, which cuts and polishes somewhere around ninety percent of the world's diamonds by piece count and sends a large slice of that output to American buyers.

What it means

  • The tariff does not fall on India so much as on the seam between India and America, and seams are where trades tear. A 10% wall around the cheapest labor in the pipeline invites the goods to route around it, and the EU carve-out draws the map.
  • For Surat, the danger is not this quarter's duty but the precedent that finishing a stone at home has become the expensive option.

The busiest lane takes the hit

The 10% duty that took effect on July 24 is not the highest rate on the board, but it lands on the single busiest lane in the polished trade, and it lands at a moment when the men who own the wheels in Surat have the least room to give.

That thin room is the second story inside the first. Rough is cheap, with De Beers' realised price down about a third year on year, but cheap rough has not rescued cutters, because polished prices have fallen alongside it and the spread that pays a factory's wages has stayed narrow.

Surat spent the past year trimming shifts and idling benches; a fresh 10% at the border is a cost that has to be split three ways, among the exporter who books the sale, the American importer who takes the goods, and the shopper who eventually pays the ticket.

The exemption draws a map around India

The exemption for European-polished stones sharpens the choice. A diamond that leaves India as rough, travels to Antwerp to be polished and enters the United States as EU goods can, on paper, sidestep the duty that the same stone would carry if finished in Gujarat.

Re-routing at scale is neither quick nor free, and rules of origin will be argued over for months, but the incentive now points away from the world's largest cutting center, which is precisely the outcome its exporters feared.

Someone along the chain holds the difference

India's trade bodies have spent the season lobbying against exactly this, arguing that duties on polished goods punish a labor-intensive industry that competes on cost. The near-term arithmetic is unforgiving: at a 10% rate on a category already running on single-digit margins, some orders simply do not clear, and the ones that do move slower.

The question the trade will spend August answering is who, along that chain from bench to counter, is left holding the difference.
