£1.2 million stops short of an 8.03-carat blue's reserve

Elmwood's offered the fancy light blue in London on 1 October against an estimate of £1.5m to £2m. The highest hammer price anything in the sale did make was £13,000.

By The Auctions Desk

10 October 2026

What changed

The reserve was not met. Elmwood's 8.03-carat fancy light blue failed to sell in London. Bidding stopped at £1.2 million against a £1.5 million low estimate.

What it means

  • If you consign a coloured diamond: The bid stopped a fifth short of the low estimate, not fractionally short.
  • If you estimate for a house: Nothing else in this sale cleared above £13,000.
  • What this is not: A withdrawal, or a value for the stone.

The bid stopped short

One point two million. Bidding on the 8.03-carat fancy light blue topped off at £1.2 million, about $1.6 million, and did not meet the seller's reserve.

A fifth below. That is 80.0% of the £1.5 million low estimate, so 20.0% under it.

Forty below the high. Against the £2 million high estimate the same bid is 60.0%, so 40.0% under it.

Grading is not it. The GIA report gives even colour distribution, VS2 clarity and no fluorescence. Elmwood's calls it the third largest blue ever offered at a UK auction.

Everything else cleared four figures.

Thirteen thousand topped it. A Tiffany & Co. ring with a 1.84-carat D/VVS1 round took £13,000, inside estimate. That was the sale's highest hammer price.

Then eight thousand. An Art Deco Tutti Frutti clip brooch made £8,000 at its low estimate. A Van Cleef & Arpels vanity case took £5,800, just below its low estimate.

Two at five five. Two sapphire jewels each sold within estimate at £5,500. One is a vintage gold bracelet; the other a c.1940 platinum ring on a 5.45-carat step-cut sapphire.

Same stone, second stage

We priced it first. Carat Capital published the £1.5 million to £2 million estimate on 23 September, before the sale. This is the outcome of that same lot.

Nine days to report. The sale ran on 1 October and the result was reported on 9 October. The eight-day gap is the reporting lag, not the event.

What would change this call

A private sale at or near the reserve, which would make this a story about the room on the day rather than about demand. It would also read differently if Elmwood's published the reserve: a bid 20% under the low estimate says nothing about how far under the reserve it fell, and the house has not disclosed that figure.

Why an unsold lot is still information

An auction result is usually read off the prices that printed, which makes a no-sale look like an absence of data. It is not. A hammer price tells you what one determined bidder would pay on one afternoon; a failed lot with published bidding tells you where the bidding actually ran out, which is a floor on demand rather than a ceiling on it. The useful number here is therefore not the estimate but the £1.2 million, because that is an amount somebody was willing to commit. The limitation is the reserve. A reserve is a private instruction between consignor and house and is almost never published, so a reader can see how far the bid fell below the printed estimate but not how far it fell below the number that would have released the stone. Those are different distances and only one of them is knowable from outside. The second thing to hold onto is the catalogue. A single seven-figure lot in a sale whose other top five prints run £5,500 to £13,000 draws a different bidder population than the same stone in a sale where the second lot is six figures, because the specialist buyers for a large fancy-colour diamond are not reliably in the room for a £5,800 vanity case.
