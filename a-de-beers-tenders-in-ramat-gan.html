Diamonds

De Beers tenders rough in Ramat Gan after nine years

Hon reports the two-day sale ran on 14 and 15 October at the exchange in Ramat Gan. Days earlier the bourse and Israel's Tax Authority signed a flat turnover-tax agreement covering 2027 and 2028, replacing a minimum-income floor.

By Carat Capital

17 October 2026

What changed

De Beers tendered in Israel again. Rough went in front of buyers at the Ramat Gan exchange on 14 and 15 October, after nine years without such a sale. Days earlier the bourse signed a 2027-28 turnover-tax deal.

What it means

  • If you trade on the Ramat Gan floor: A flat charge on turnover replaces the minimum-income floor for anyone who joins. The rate is not published, so the sum cannot yet be done.
  • If you are weighing Antwerp against Dubai: Israel now offers a known tax bill plus duty-free entry for gems, against 5% customs in Dubai.
  • What this is not: A result. No lots, carats, prices or signatures have been reported from the tender, and Israel's own tax rate has not been printed by anyone.

The tender has already run

Two days, now past. Hon dated its report 13 October Israel time and placed the goods on the table from the following day until Thursday. That is 14 and 15 October.

No result exists. Neither De Beers nor the exchange has published a lot count, a carat weight, an asking price or a sell-through. The report carries none of them either.

Nine years is theirs. The gap since the last such sale in Israel is Hon's own count. Carat Capital has not matched it against a De Beers sight calendar.

The tax deal has a date

A floor is cancelled. A dealer who joins the arrangement pays a fixed percentage of turnover. The minimum-income floor, which forced a set reported income in thin years, goes for entrants.

The rate is withheld. Hon prints Belgium's 2.1% of turnover and calls it more than three times Israel's figure. It never prints the Israeli number itself.

Two years, not more. The agreement covers the 2027 and 2028 tax years. Nothing in the report says what happens after that or who is eligible to elect in.

The competition is the point

Duty is another lever. Gems and jewellery enter Israel free of customs duty against 5% in Dubai, and Hon describes foreign company registration as faster in Israel.

Volume wins, margin loses. A flat charge on turnover favours a house that turns stock fast at a thin margin and penalises one that trades a small book wide.

The test is unanswered. Whether buyers signed at the tender is what the two days were for. From outside, a sale that drew lookers reads the same as one that cleared.

What to watch

  • Any day nowA De Beers or exchange statement giving the tender's result. Nothing has been issued since the sale closed on 15 October.
  • Before 1 January 2027Publication of the Israeli flat rate. Until it exists, no dealer outside the arrangement can price the choice.
  • OngoingWhether Antwerp or Dubai answers. Both have competed for the same dealer base through 2026.

What would change this call

A second Israeli outlet, or the exchange's own statement. This rests on one Hebrew financial title. The tender dates are derived from the report's dateline rather than stated as calendar dates in the text, and if Hon published later than its JSON-LD timestamp says, the two days shift. A published Israeli rate would also change the reading entirely: a flat charge that lands near 0.7% of turnover is a different proposition from one near 0.2%, and nothing available tonight distinguishes them.

What a minimum-income floor does to a diamond book

Israel has long taxed diamond dealers against a floor rather than purely against their accounts. The mechanism obliges a dealer to report at least a set level of income, calculated by formula, whatever the books say. In a strong year the floor is irrelevant because real profit clears it. In a weak year it becomes the whole tax bill, and it arrives in exactly the year a dealer can least afford it. That asymmetry is why the floor has been a grievance in Ramat Gan through a two-year downturn in which polished exports fell by a fifth. Replacing it with a flat percentage of turnover does two things at once. It makes the bill predictable, because turnover is known monthly and a percentage of it can be budgeted. And it moves the burden from profit to volume, which rewards a trading house that moves goods quickly and thin, and punishes a house that holds inventory and sells a few stones at a wide spread. Belgium made the same move years ago and settled at 2.1% of turnover as deemed taxable income, which is the comparator Hon reaches for. The Israeli number is the thing that decides whether this is a concession or a restructuring, and it has not been published.
